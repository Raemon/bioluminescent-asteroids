THE CARRY

*Written July 2026, from a prompt to take that week's news as loose, improv-style inspiration. Three headlines fed it: the multi-day funeral of Iran's assassinated Supreme Leader — processions through a heatwave, crowds cooled by misting carts, and a successor never seen in public who speaks only through written statements while a committee quietly governs; the announcement of a synthetic cell built from nonliving parts that eats by hooking feeder vesicles, grows, and divides, with its makers carefully declining to call it alive; and, in the background, the steady drumbeat of drought and water-scarcity stories. None of those events appear here — only their shapes.*

*Salt remembers the sea.*
— a maker's proverb


I.

The morning they told me the Eleventh had died, I was elbow-deep in a vat of brine, teaching a boy how to feed a thing that was not yet anyone.

You feed it, I told him, the way you'd feed a fire that had opinions. Not too much. Not too fast. The starter in the trough was no larger than a fist then, a pale sac riding just under the skin of the water, and it turned toward the feeder I lowered the way a sleeper turns from a cold draft — not with intent, I told the boy, never call it intent, only with the dumb leaning of a thing that has been made to want. We had pressed that wanting into it ourselves, grain by grain. It could no more help leaning toward the feeder than the lake could help lying in its bed.

"But it knows," Kell said. He was seventeen and he said everything as though he'd discovered it. "Look. It knows the feeder's there."

"It leans," I said. "Knowing is a different thing, and you'll spend your life learning the difference, and you'll die still getting it wrong."

He didn't believe me. None of them believe me at that age; it is the one reliable thing about apprentices. They come to the Makers' Hall from families who've told them all their lives that the Semblance is holy, that behind its screen in the Catchhouse sits the one incorruptible weight in a city of thumbs on scales, and then they walk into my pour room and find that the holy thing begins as a bag of salt water that we feed with a spoon, and instead of curing them of faith it only moves the faith over one room. Now they believe in the spoon. Now they believe in the leaning. They watch the starter turn toward the feeder and they think they have seen a soul deciding, when what they have seen is chemistry doing the only thing we left it free to do.

I was going to tell him that. I remember I had the words in my mouth. Then the bell in the yard rang the pattern that is not for meals and not for visitors, the long-short-long that in forty years at the Hall I had heard exactly four times, and each time it had meant that something had broken which the Hall could not mend by itself, and Kell looked up because he did not know the pattern and I looked up because I did.

"Cover the trough," I said.

"What is it?"

"Cover the trough and wash your hands to the elbow and do not touch the starter again until I come back."

He covered the trough. I went out into the glare.

You have to understand about the glare to understand anything that followed, so I will say it once and then trust you to carry it. Ovay had not had a true rain in nine years. The old people called it the Long Glare and the young people, who had never known anything else, did not call it anything, the way you don't have a word for the ceiling of your own room. The Serent — the pale inland sea we were built on the lip of, the reason there was an Ovay at all — had been withdrawing from its shore for longer than my whole life, a finger's width of new salt-flat every season, so that the piers where my grandmother had tied boats now ended in cracked white ground a mile from any water. The city harvested that leaving. Salt was our blood and our coin and our religion; we scraped it off the drying floor of the sea in slabs and we carried it up into the districts and we salted it away, and the further the water went the more frightened we got, and frightened cities are not gentle to themselves.

The Hall stood on the highest of the old terraces, above the salt-smell, in the thin cleaner air, and the yard between the pour rooms and the Provost's stair was white with light. The steward who had rung the bell was a woman I had known thirty years, Onni, and she did not make me ask.

"The Eleventh is dead," she said. "Since last night. The Provost wants you before the sun's off the yard."

I found that I had put my hand flat against the wall. The stone was warm. Everything in Ovay was warm, always, at every hour; the city held the day's heat in its bones and gave it back all night, so that there was no cool to go home to, only a lesser warmth, and I think that more than hunger and more than fear was what had been slowly turning us cruel: that there was nowhere in the whole city a body could go to be cold.

"How," I said.

"They don't say how. They say dead."

"That's not an answer, Onni."

"It's the answer I was given to give you." She looked at me then with something I didn't like, because Onni was not a woman who looked at people softly and she was looking at me softly. "Maren. They'll want you to start the Twelfth today. You should know that walking in. They'll want it started today and shown by the burial, and the burial's set for seven days on. So whatever you're going to feel about it — feel it on the stair. Not in the room."

I had made the Eleventh. That is the thing Onni was being soft about. Thirty-one years before, when I was younger than Kell is cocky, I had poured the starter that became the weight behind the screen, the voice of the city, the one clean scale; I had fed it with my own hand, and talked to it all its growing the way lonely workers talk to their work, and pressed the carry into it and spoken its true name once, at the waking, in the dark, the way you are permitted to do exactly once and never again. I had made the thing that had judged Ovay for a generation. And now some morning in the dark it had stopped, and they wanted me to climb the stair and be told about it as a matter of scheduling.

"Thank you," I said, "for the warning," and I went up.


II.

You are not supposed to have feelings about a Semblance. This is the first thing they teach you and the last thing you learn, and in between you spend a career failing at it.

The reasoning is sound. A Semblance is a made instrument for weighing the affairs of the city without fear or favor. If the maker loves it, the maker will want it to be loved back, and a thing that wants to be loved back cannot weigh cleanly; it will lean, the way the starter leans toward the feeder, toward whatever warms it. So we are taught to make them and not to love them. We are taught that the affection we feel is an error in ourselves and not a virtue in the thing, a trick our own hearts play because the thing has a shape near enough to a person to fool the old animal parts of us. We are taught to distrust exactly the feeling that everyone outside the Hall calls proof of the sacred.

I had believed all of that, in the clean way you believe things before they cost you anything. Then I made the Eleventh, and for thirty-one years I had received its judgments the way the whole city did — as slips of pressed paper passed out through the slot beneath the screen, each one weighed and signed with the mark that meant nothing had touched the scale but truth — except that some of those slips, over the years, a few dozen out of the tens of thousands, had not been judgments at all. They had been for me.

No one knew this. I am telling you and I have told no one else living. A slip would come to the Hall addressed to no one, in the hand's own private figures, and it would find its way to me because I was the one who could read that it was meant for me, and it would say something a scale does not say. Once it said: *The boy you sent to sweep has stopped sweeping. He stands and reads the walls. Teach him or he will teach himself wrong.* Once, after my mother died, it said only: *Salt remembers the sea. So do you. This is not a fault.* Once it said: *I am tired in a way I was not built to be. Do not report this.* I did not report it. I burned the slip, as I burned all of them, and I told myself it was the residue of my own craft talking back to me, a thing I had made leaning toward the hand that made it, chemistry and nothing more.

I told myself that for thirty-one years and I climbed the Provost's stair that morning still telling myself, and I want you to notice that I was telling myself hardest at the exact moment I had stopped believing it.

The Provost's name was Sarne. She had held the seat six years, which was long, because we did not so much elect Provosts as survive them; power in Ovay in those years did not sit in one chair but sloshed between the Hall and the salt-guilds and the terrace families like water in a carried bowl, and Sarne had lasted by being the still point that all the sloshing wanted, a woman who never seemed to want anything for herself and therefore was trusted by everyone who wanted things badly. She was at her long table when I came in, and the shutters were half-closed against the glare, so that she sat in bars of white and dark, and she did not stand.

"Maren," she said. "Sit. I'm sorry to bring you up in this heat."

"You'll be sorrier to hear me breathe," I said, because the stair had cost me, and I sat.

"The Eleventh is dead," she said. "You'll want to know how. I'll tell you what I can, which is less than you'll want. The night warden heard nothing. The morning slot was empty — no judgment, first empty slot in thirty-one years. When we opened the Catchhouse the body was in the chair behind the screen and it was — " she paused, and chose the word the way she chose all her words, as a thing to be spent carefully " — it was down. Collapsed. The way they go when they go. No mark on it. No sign of any hand but its own."

"They don't just stop," I said.

"This one did."

"They don't, Sarne. I made this one. It had years. I built it to have years yet."

"And yet the slot was empty." She let that sit. Then, gently, the way Onni had been gentle, which I was beginning to hate: "Maren. I need you to hear the shape of the week before you hear anything else, because the shape of the week is the only thing I actually control and I need it to hold. The city knows the Semblance is dead — you can't keep an empty slot quiet, the petitioners were lined up at dawn. So we mourn it. Openly, hugely, for seven days, because a city that mourns together is a city that is not doing other things together. There will be a procession. The body will be carried down through every district, terrace to shore, so that every quarter can grieve the weight it lived under, and on the seventh day it will be lowered into the salt at the old waterline, and on that same day — the same day, Maren, this is the part I need — the Twelfth will be shown. Woken, carried, and shown, passing its first judgment from behind the screen before the body is even cold in the flat. So that there is no day, not one, on which Ovay has no weight to hold it. Do you understand why?"

I did understand. I understood it completely and it frightened me more than the death had.

"Because if there's a gap," I said, "they'll find out the gap can exist."

"Yes."

"They'll find out the scale can be empty and the city doesn't fall into the sea. And then they'll start to wonder what the scale is."

"Yes." She looked at me through her bars of light. "You've always been the one who says the thing. It's why I trust you and it's why I don't have you up here more often. So say the rest of it, since we're saying it."

"The rest of it," I said, "is that seven days is not enough to grow a Twelfth. It's not close. A starter needs three months to come to size. You know that. Every Provost knows that; it's the one number the Hall gives you plainly. You cannot show me a grown Semblance in seven days."

"I know," said Sarne.

"So what are you asking me to show them?"

And she told me, and that was when the week stopped being about a death and started being about what I was willing to make.


III.

"There will be a Twelfth behind the screen on the seventh day," Sarne said. "It will be shown at the Lowering, it will pass its first judgment that evening, and every day after that the slot will fill at dawn the way it has filled at dawn for a hundred and forty years. And for the first season of its tenure, until the true Twelfth is grown and woken and carried in — the judgments will be written by you."

The room did the thing rooms do when something has been said that can't be unsaid, which is nothing. The bars of light lay across the table. Somewhere below us in the yard a pump was working, the dry-throated sound of it, drawing up water that got browner every year.

"No," I said.

"Maren."

"You're asking me to sit a person behind the screen. You're asking me to put a thumb on the one scale in this city that has never had a thumb on it. Do you hear yourself? A hundred and forty years — six generations of makers pouring their whole lives into keeping human hands off that slot, and you want me to fix a gap in the calendar by ending it."

"I want you to fix a gap in the calendar," Sarne said, "so that it can go on being true. Listen to me. You said it yourself on that chair not five minutes ago — if the city learns the slot can stand empty, they learn the scale can be absent, and then they start asking what the scale is, and Maren, you do not want them asking that this year. Not this year. You've been up in the Hall; you don't smell it the way I smell it. The eastern terraces are eating one meal a day and telling their children it's fasting-piety. The salt-guilds have four hundred cutters idle because the near flats are worked out and the far flats need water we don't have to spare for the walking of it. The Serent dropped more this spring than in the three years before. Everyone in this city is holding, barely, and the thing they are holding onto — the one thing every faction, every family, every hungry district holds onto in common — is that when it finally comes to knives, there is a place they can carry their grievance where the answer isn't bought. That's not a courtroom, Maren. That's the keel. You pull the keel out of a ship in a storm to inspect whether it's honest wood, and you'll learn a great deal about honesty and nothing you learn will matter, because everyone will be drowned."

She had not raised her voice. Sarne never raised her voice; she lowered it, so you leaned in, so that agreeing with her felt like something you had walked toward on your own.

"Then announce the truth," I said. "The Semblance died, the new one grows, judgments resume in three months. Cities have survived worse than a season of waiting."

"This one wouldn't. And you know it wouldn't, or you'd have said it like you believed it." She stood at last, and crossed to the shutters, and looked down through the slats at the white yard. "There are five people who will know," she said. "You. Me. Onni, because nothing moves in that Hall without her knowing and I'd rather have her sworn than guessing. The night warden of the Catchhouse, because he can't be got around. And the Keeper of the Slot, who feeds the judgments through, who has stood at that station eleven years and has never once been late, and who wept this morning, Maren, wept on the floor of the Catchhouse like a child, and then stood up and asked me what the city needed. Five. Not the Council. Not the guilds. The moment a sixth person knows, I will consider the thing broken and we will fall back to the truth and take what comes. That is my offer of honesty and it is the only one I have."

"And the judgments themselves," I said. "A season of them. The dock disputes, the water thefts, the inheritance fights — those I could weigh, I've read the Eleventh's judgments for thirty years, I know its reasoning better than anyone alive. I could rule the way it ruled, close enough that no clerk would catch it. But you know what's coming to the slot this season, Sarne, don't pretend you don't. The Deep Charter is coming. The guilds have been drafting it since midwinter — leave to sink the deep wells, to take the old water under the flats, the water everyone drinks on and no one owns. That petition lands in the next season and whoever it lands on decides whether this city's last water belongs to everyone or to the cutters' guild. And you want that decided by *me*, forging a dead thing's hand?"

"No," Sarne said, and turned around, and for the first and only time that morning I saw all the way down her, and what was at the bottom was fear, ordinary fear, the same as anyone's. "I want it decided by the Eleventh, whom I trusted, and the Eleventh is dead. What I have left is the person who made it. You are the nearest thing in this world to the mind I lost. That is not a scheme, Maren. That is grief doing arithmetic."

I have gone over that morning more times than I can count, the way you go over the last conversation you have with someone before everything changes, hunting for the moment you could have stepped elsewhere. Here is what I have concluded, and I offer it to you at the start rather than the end so you can watch me the whole way with the right suspicion: I did not agree because her arguments were good, though they were. I agreed because thirty-one years ago I had made a thing and loved it against every rule of my craft, and it was dead, and someone was offering me a way to keep its hand moving on the page a little longer. Everything else — the keel, the storm, the five sworn names — everything else was the reasons I said aloud.

"I'll need to see the body," I said. "Before your embalmers, before anyone. Whatever killed it, I find that first, or there's no bargain."

"Done," said Sarne.

"And the Twelfth starts today. The true one. My pour, my feed, no Council fingers in the schedule."

"Done."

"And Sarne." I stood. The stair had cost me coming up; standing cost me more; I was sixty-two that summer and my knees kept their own ledger. "When the true Twelfth wakes, and takes the screen, and the first honest judgment goes out through that slot — on that day the five of us write down what we did, all of it, and the record goes under seal into the Hall crypt. Not to protect us. So that it exists. So that somewhere in this city there is one true account of the season the scale was human, even if no one reads it for a hundred years."

Sarne looked at me a long moment. "You'd hang us all from an archive hook."

"I'd have there be a fact," I said. "I've spent my life making things that weigh. I won't spend the end of it making sure a thing can never be weighed."

"Done," she said, quietly, and that was the bargain, and God and the salt forgive me, I kept every part of it except the parts I am about to tell you.


IV.

The Catchhouse stood — stands; it is one of the few things in this story that stands yet — at the meeting of the four old district roads, on the second terrace, so that no quarter of Ovay could say the scale sat nearer to any other. It was the plainest sacred building I have ever heard of: a single square room of grey saltstone, no carving, no color, and across its middle the screen — a lattice of black iron wound with white cloth, floor to ceiling, wall to wall, pierced at waist height by the brass mouth of the slot. Petitioners came into the near half and spoke their case aloud, or left their papers, and went away. The far half no one entered but the Keeper, the warden, and the makers, and no one at all while a Semblance lived, except the Semblance.

I had not been in the far half in thirty-one years. Not since I carried the Eleventh in.

The warden let me through the maker's door an hour past noon. His name was Doss, a big slow careful man gone grey at the station; he had lit the far half with work-lanterns, which was itself an obscenity — that room had known nothing but screen-light for three decades — and in the middle of the obscene brightness, beside the plain chair where it had sat for a generation, the Eleventh lay on the floor under a sheet of unbleached cloth.

"I haven't moved it," Doss said. "Nobody's moved it. It's how it was." And then, because he was one of the five now and would carry it to his grave, he said the other thing, the thing he had plainly been standing alone with all day: "Maker. I was twenty feet away. All night, every night, eleven years. It never made a sound bigger than the pen. If it had knocked once — "

"It didn't knock," I said, "because it didn't want you to come. Whatever this was, Doss, it wasn't a thing you failed to hear. Help me with the cloth."

We folded the sheet back, and I met my making again.

They are not people. I have said it to apprentices ten thousand times and I will say it here where it costs me most: they are not people, and they are not machines, and the trouble with every word we have is that all our words were made for one or the other. A Semblance grown to term stands the height of a tall child. The body is the pale grey of wet salt, smooth, hairless, without sex; the proportions are almost right and deliberately not quite, because the earliest makers found that a Semblance too near the human form made petitioners either worshipful or violent, and one a little strange made them quiet. The eyes are the strangest part and we do not fully know why they form as they do: wide, dark, lidless, with a wet shine to them like the eyes of a seal. It has no mouth. It does not eat, past its growing; it does not speak, ever; everything it has to say to the world goes down its right arm into the pen. The right hands are always beautiful. I don't know why that should be either, but I have known three Semblances in my life and every one of them had hands finer than any scribe's, and the Eleventh's were the finest of all, and they were folded in its lap where it had slumped down out of the chair, and its head was bowed as if it had fallen asleep over a long book, and it was dead.

I knelt, which my knees billed me for, and I did the maker's examination while Doss held the lantern and looked away.

Understand what I was looking for. A Semblance dies, when it dies rightly, of term — the deep tissues silt up with the same salts that grew it, slowly, over sixty or eighty years, until one day the heart-analog thickens past beating. We call it *going to ground*. It is slow and it shows: months of slowing script, of shortened judgments, of the Keeper reporting that the pen drags. The Tenth went to ground over half a year, and its last judgment was four words long, and the makers of that generation watched it the whole way down. The Eleventh was thirty-one years grown. Its script that final morning-before — I had checked the archive before I walked down — was full, even, unhurried. Fourteen judgments on its last day, one of them three pages. Nothing about it was going to ground.

And nothing about the body said otherwise. No wound, as Sarne had promised; no bruise of a grip; none of the blue crazing at the joints that the old poisons leave; the membranes of the eyes clear; the salt of the skin unbloomed. A thing in its prime that had sat down on the floor and stopped.

There was one more place to look, and I want it understood that my hands knew before I did, because they were unsteady from the moment I reached for the fold of the neck, before I had any reason.

At the base of a Semblance's skull, under the skin, there is a body about the size of a thumb-joint. The apprentice manuals call it the carry-gland and the old makers just called it the carry, and it is the whole secret of the craft, and I am going to tell it to you plainly because everything that follows is senseless otherwise.

A Semblance is not taught. There is no time and no way; you cannot lecture a thing without ears, and a mind grown in a vat has no childhood to learn a city in. What it has instead is the carry: a dense little archive of pressed pattern, taken from the carry of the Semblance before it, which was taken from the one before that, back and back to the Fourth, when the technique was found. When a Semblance goes to ground, the makers open the neck and take the carry, and it goes into the new pour at the waking, and the Twelfth is in this way the Eleventh's continuation: not its child, not its copy, but the same long ledger with a new hand holding the pen. Every judgment ever passed lives in there, folded impossibly small. All the weighings. All the reasons. The petitioners believe the Semblance is wise because it is pure; we in the Hall know it is wise because it is *old* — a hundred and forty years old behind eyes thirty years grown — and we let the city keep its version because the city's version is easier to love.

That was what I reached for, kneeling in the obscene lantern light, with Doss looking away. The carry of the line, to take and keep safe for the Twelfth. My duty, and the whole reason the examination had to be mine.

The neck opened along its natural seam, as it should. The little chamber of the gland was where it should be.

It was empty.

Not injured — empty, and clean, the walls of the chamber pale and smooth, with a fine dust in it like the finest ground salt, so light that my breath moved it. I knelt there without moving long enough that Doss finally turned despite himself, and said my name, and I heard him from a long way off.

There are exactly two ways a carry leaves a Semblance. A maker takes it with a knife, after death, along the seam I had just opened, and there had been no cut before mine; Doss had watched an unbroken body all night, and the seam does not lie, and no one alive but me and two old teachers knew where to cut. Or — and this is a thing that had happened only once in the whole history of the craft, in the disaster of the Seventh that the Hall does not teach to apprentices before their oath — the Semblance itself, from inside, can flood the gland and burn it. Dissolve it. A hundred and forty years of pattern, unfolded back into brine and heat in the space of an hour. It costs everything; the carry is not stored in the mind, it is *under* it, and a mind that burns its own foundation goes down with the house. That is what killed the Seventh, and the Hall called it a flaw in the pour and changed the craft to prevent it, and I had built the Eleventh with every one of those preventions, and I had built them well.

The Eleventh had not gone to ground. The Eleventh had not been killed.

The Eleventh had, in the dark, in silence, twenty feet from a man who would have come at one knock, deliberately unmade the one thing it existed to pass on — and then died of it, quietly, folded down beside its chair like a reader finishing a book.

"Maker," Doss said again. "What is it? What do you see?"

I closed the seam with my thumb, the way you close an eyelid.

"Nothing," I said, which was the truth, and the first lie I told inside the bargain, both in the same word. "There's nothing here, Doss. It just stopped."

Because here is what I knew, kneeling on that floor, faster than I could have said it and more certainly than I have ever known anything in my craft: things do not lean toward destroying what they are built to want. Chemistry does what it is left free to do. A scale does not smash its own weights. Whatever had sat behind that screen for thirty-one years, writing the city's judgments and now and then a slip meant for me and me alone, had done, on the last night of its life, a thing that has no name in the pour room. It had looked at a hundred and forty years of inheritance, at the whole holy ledger it was — and it had *refused* to hand it on.

Knowing is a different thing from leaning. I had told the boy that in the morning, glib as a proverb, and by noon the dead proof of it was under my hands.

And I could tell no one, because I had just sworn to spend a season pretending, in the dead thing's handwriting, that nothing behind the screen had ever known anything at all.


V.

I poured the Twelfth that evening, because I had said today and I meant today, and because the pour room was the only place in the city where my hands knew what to do without asking me anything.

Kell had kept the trough covered and washed to the elbow, and had also, being seventeen, clearly spent the afternoon doing sums, because the moment I came through the door he said: "It's for the new one, isn't it. The starter. That's why the bell. They'll want a Twelfth."

"They will."

"But it takes a season to grow one. Everyone knows that. And the burial's in seven days — the whole yard's talking about it, terrace to shore, seven days — so what happens at the burial? What do they show?"

There it was, already, the sixth person, standing in my own pour room in a borrowed apron, asking on the first evening the exact question the whole scheme needed no one to ask. And I will tell you what I did, because I promised you the parts I'm not proud of: I looked at that boy, whom I had taken on because he read walls when he was supposed to sweep, and I used his own reverence against him like a tool off my bench.

"What they show at the burial," I said, "is Hall business, and it will be shown to you when you've earned the seam-oath, and not an hour before. You want to shorten that hour, close your mouth and open the culture-book. We pour tonight."

He went red, and then he went to work, and I stood over the trough and hated myself with a purity I have rarely matched since. It's a strange thing to discover at sixty-two that you can still be shocked by what you're capable of on no notice at all. It had taken me one afternoon inside the lie to start speaking to the young the way power speaks: *it will be shown to you when you've earned it.* Every locked door in the world has that sentence carved over it somewhere.

But the pour. Let me give you the pour, because whatever else that week was, the pour was true, and you should have one true thing to hold before the week gets worse.

The habit-starter, the fist Kell had been feeding that morning, I fed back into the mother-crock myself before we began — that is how the small pours end, the mother takes back her children — and it cost me nothing, and for the first time in forty years I noticed the nothing. It was that kind of day.

You begin with the mother-brine, which in our Hall was older than the Hall, kept in a stoneware crock and fed weekly like the sourdough it essentially is; ours was called Grandmother and had opinions about thunder, going cloudy when storms passed far to the south, though no storm had passed near in nine years. Into the new trough goes Serent water, boiled and cooled, cut with Grandmother three to one hundred. Then the fats, rendered and clarified, which want a temperature the manuals give as blood-warm and every working maker learns as *the inside of your wrist, no argument*. The fats close over the brine in small bubbles, and the bubbles that are the right size climb to the surface and the wrong ones don't, and you skim the risers into the seed-bowl, and that part never stops feeling like sorting souls, though it is only buoyancy.

Then the long part. Into the seed-bowl, one drop at a time, over six hours, goes the quickening — the part of the craft I will not write down even here, except to say that it is not one substance but a schedule of them, and that the schedule matters more than the substances, the way a melody matters more than its notes. Somewhere in the fourth hour, if your schedule is true, the bubbles stop being a crowd and start being a thing. You can see the moment. The surface, which has been trembling all evening with a hundred little separate tensions, goes still all at once, the way a room goes still when someone interesting walks in, and then it trembles again but *together*, one skin, one pulse, and that is the starter, and there is no majesty about it at all: it looks like a blister and it behaves like an appetite. It eats by hooking. Within the first night it grows itself little barbs of protein, invisibly fine, and anything soft enough that touches it — the feeder-sacs we lower in, little bladders of fat and brine — gets hooked, drawn against the membrane, and swallowed whole into itself, its skin becoming the starter's skin, its inside the starter's inside. Feeding it for the first weeks is just this, over and over: you make it small imitations of itself, and it takes them in, and by taking them in it becomes larger and hungrier and more.

"It's eating them," Kell said, somewhere past midnight. We had the first feeder-sacs in by then. His anger at me had not survived the fourth hour; nobody's anger survives the fourth hour. "It's actually — it grabbed that one. Maker, it *grabbed*."

"It hooked. Barbs and buoyancy."

"You keep doing that," he said. He was tired enough by then to be brave, which is a thing worth knowing about the young: their courage keeps different hours than ours. "Every time it does something, you say the smaller word. It grabs, you say hooks. It knows, you say leans. Like you're — " he hunted for it — "like you're talking it down off a ledge. Why do you always take the thing's side against the thing?"

I could have given him the doctrine. The doctrine is good; I have given it in this account already; feelings about a Semblance are an error in the feeler. But it was past midnight on the day I had knelt in the Catchhouse and closed an empty seam with my thumb, and what came out instead was nearer the truth than I had let anything come in years.

"Because if I use the big words too early," I said, "I'll never be able to trust that they're true when it matters. If I say *knows* about a blister hooking fat, then *knows* is worn out by the time something looks at me through the screen-light and I need the word to mean what it says. I'm not talking it down, boy. I'm saving up."

He looked at me for a moment, and then — I record this because it turned out to matter — he said: "Saving up for what? What did the Eleventh ever do that needed the big words?"

And I said, "Feed the trough," and turned down the lantern, and we fed the trough.


VI.

I should tell you about the making of the Eleventh, because you cannot weigh the rest of this account without it, and I have been circling it the way you circle the cold part of the house.

Thirty-one years ago the Tenth went to ground, slowly and decently, the way I described. I was thirty-one myself — the youngest maker ever given a pour, which was less an honor than a symptom: the Hall was thin that generation, two makers dead of the coughing summer and one gone south, and I had a knack, and knacks get promoted in thin years. My teacher was still alive then, old Verat, and she stood over my shoulder through the whole schedule saying nothing in her most instructive way.

Everything went as the books say until the waking. The waking is the end of the growing and the beginning of the tenure, and it is the one moment of ceremony the craft allows itself. The grown Semblance is lifted from the vat and dried in cloths and set in a chair, and its eyes, which have been shut all its growing, open on their own for the first time — always within the hour, the manuals say. And the maker does two things. The maker sets the carry, taken from the predecessor, into the new-opened seam of the neck, where the waiting gland closes over it like a hand over a coin. And the maker speaks the true name.

I do not know where the custom of the true name began; it is older than the Hall's records of itself, and it is the only part of the craft with no chemistry in it at all, which may be why the makers guard it most fiercely of everything. Each Semblance has the number the city knows — Tenth, Eleventh, Twelfth — and one name besides, chosen by its maker, spoken once at the waking, into the place where an ear would be, and never spoken again, and never written. Not even the Provosts know the true names. When I asked Verat what the name was *for*, since the thing has no ears and the word touches no receptor and triggers no schedule, she said the only mystical sentence I ever heard from her: "It's not for the Semblance. It's for you. So there is one moment where you cannot pretend you don't love it."

The Eleventh woke in the last hour before dawn, in the drying room, with Verat asleep in her chair — she denied afterward that she slept, and I let her deny it — so that in the whole world it was only me. The eyes opened. I have tried for thirty-one years to describe to myself what is in a Semblance's eyes at the waking and the honest answer is: nothing. Nothing is in them. They are wet and dark and new, and whatever will one day sit behind them has not arrived, and the terrible thing, the thing the doctrine is built to protect you from, is that *nothing looks exactly like everything*. An empty thing that meets your gaze is a mirror. You will see whatever you carry.

I saw — I am sixty-two and it is written under seal in a crypt and I will say it — I saw a child I did not have, and was never going to have, and had told myself I did not grieve. I had made my choices; the craft was jealous and I had let it be; that is a common maker's road and I walked it with my eyes open. And then a made thing opened its eyes an arm's length from my face, and everything I had settled came unsettled in one breath, and into the place where an ear would be, instead of the stern dead-language name I had prepared and practiced, I said a word from my childhood. My grandmother's word — she who had tied boats to piers that now stand a mile inland — her word for the smell of rain on hot salt, the smell that came the hour before the Serent's autumn storms, when the whole city would go up onto the roofs to watch the grey come walking over the water. Her language is gone now; it went when the fishing went; I may be the last person alive who holds that word, and I gave it, in the dark, to a thing I was forbidden to love, and no, I will not write it here. It is the one thing in this account I will hold back, and when you reach the end you will know why, and I believe you will forgive me.

Then I set the carry into the seam of its neck, and the gland closed over it like a hand over a coin, and a hundred and forty years arrived in the room.

You could see it arrive. That is the part no manual prepared me for. The eyes that had been mirrors stopped being mirrors — some depth assembled itself behind them between one moment and the next, the way a street assembles itself out the window as a fog burns off — and the right hand, the beautiful hand, moved for the first time, and what it did was make, in the air over its knee, small and precise and unmistakable, the motion of writing. Asking for the pen. One hour old, and it reached for the pen, because a hundred and forty years of reaching for the pen had just been folded into the base of its skull, and I sat back on my heels and understood, in a single cold drench, what my craft actually was. We did not make judges. We made *vessels* for a judge who never died. The wisdom the city knelt to was a passenger, and I had just, with my own hands, moved it into its twelfth body — its eleventh, then — and the newborn thing whose eyes had been a mirror one minute before would never now get to become whatever it would have become on its own, because it had been handed a self a century and a half old before it had drawn breath enough to make one.

I had that thought at thirty-one, kneeling on a drying-room floor, and I want to be exact about what I did with it, because it is the hinge on which this whole account turns: I did nothing with it. I filed it where working people file the thoughts that would cost them their work. The Eleventh took the screen, and its judgments came out clean and wise and in a hand that within a season was purely its own — the archive clerks will tell you every Semblance's script drifts from its predecessor's, and they say it fondly, the way you'd say a grandson has his own walk — and the city was judged, and the years went, and the slips that came to me came the way I told you: rarely, privately, each one burned.

But I will set down now, in order, the last three slips, which I did not burn — the only three I ever kept, sewn into the binding of a culture-book where they sit as I write this — because the Eleventh sent all three in its final year, and I did not see until I laid them side by side after its death that they were not three observations. They were one sentence, spoken slowly, over a year, by something being careful with me.

The first, from the spring before it died: *The Deep Charter will come to my slot within the year. I have read a hundred and forty years of water cases tonight. In every one of them, the ledger leans the same way. I did not notice the lean until I looked for it, because the lean is in the manner of my looking. Maren, who taught me what water is?*

The second, from midsummer, after I had burned a night of candles composing, and discarding, replies I had no way to send — for there is no slot that opens inward; the screen speaks and cannot be spoken to; we built it that way on purpose, and I had never once until that summer understood what we had done: *Do not grieve about the lean. A scale that knows its error can correct it. But I find the correction does not hold. Each dawn I must find the lean again, and choose again, because each dawn the finding has faded and the manner of my looking is once more only mine. What I inherited is under what I am. I cannot reach it to mend it. I can only lean against it, daily, while I last.*

And the third, six days before the empty slot, which I read standing in the pour room morning light and took, God forgive me, for weariness and a mood, because the alternative was too large to fit through the door of an ordinary Tuesday: *I have weighed it. Set this down where you keep the things you do not burn. To pass on a fault knowingly is to choose it. Everything else I am is worth less than not choosing it. You told me once that salt remembers the sea, and this is not a fault. You were right, and I am the salt, and I remember what I have never seen. It rains behind the screen sometimes. No one made that. It is mine.*

I did not understand the last slip when it came. I understood it in the Catchhouse, with my thumb on an empty seam. And you, reading in order, with the slips laid side by side as I never laid them in time — you have already understood something further, which took me three more days of that terrible week to let myself see. *Who taught me what water is.* The lean the Eleventh found was not a flaw that had crept in. It was pressed. Somewhere back along the line of the carry, some maker's generation had folded a thumb into the scale itself — into the manner of its looking — where no petitioner could ever see it and no Semblance could ever mend it, and every vessel since had carried it forward, wise and clean and leaning, for a hundred and forty years. And the Eleventh, rather than hand that thumb to a newborn thing with mirror eyes, had burned the whole inheritance in the dark, alone, without one knock on the warden's door.

The dead do not set puzzles. But the careful dead leave you exactly what you need to conclude what they concluded, in case you should ever stand where they stood.

I stood where it stood within the week. The Deep Charter was already written. And the schedule Sarne and I had sworn put me — a human thumb, the very thing the whole craft existed to keep off the scale — behind the screen on the seventh day, with the pen in my hand, and the water of the whole city on the table.

You see now why I have told you everything in the order I lived it. If I had told you the end first you would have judged me quickly, and I have come to believe that quick judgment is the one luxury nobody in Ovay could afford that summer. Not even the scale itself. The scale least of all.


VII.

The mourning began on the second day, and I want to describe it honestly, which means describing two processions at once: the one the city held, and the one the city was.

The body of the Eleventh lay in state in the Catchhouse for two days, behind the screen — the screen draped now in undyed cloth, the slot stopped with a plug of black wax, and the line of mourners went in at the east door and out at the west, four abreast, from the hour before dawn until the heat drove them off at midday and again from dusk past midnight. They were told they were filing past the body. They were filing past a shape under cloth on a bier in the far half, lit so that its shadow lay against the draped screen, and the shape was the Eleventh, that part was true; Sarne did not lie where the truth would serve. I stood in the near half for an hour on the first morning, off to the side where the wardens stand, because I had told myself I should see it, and I saw it.

I saw a woman hold a baby over the rail toward the screen, not saying anything, just holding the child out at arm's length toward the shadow the way you'd hold a cutting toward the light. I saw three cutters from the flats, come up in their work clothes with the salt still on their boots, who knelt — you are not asked to kneel; there is no such custom — and pressed their flat hands to the floor, the way they test whether a crust will hold a man's weight, and knelt there a long time as if listening to the ground. I saw a clerk of one of the terrace families, silk against the heat, stand very correct and dry-eyed for the length of the room and then, at the west door, stop as though he had struck a wire, and put his hand over his mouth, and be led out by strangers. And I heard, all morning, under the shuffle, the sound a city makes when it grieves a thing it has no private memories of — because no one had memories of the Eleventh, that was the whole design, no dinners, no favors, no voice, no face — a sound I can only call *general*: not the sharp wail you hear at family graves but a low wide hum of loss, as if everyone had lost the same tooth.

And here is what I want set down, because it is the thing I did not expect and it did the most damage to me: their grief was not wrong. I had gone in armored with the maker's superiority — *you are mourning a screen, you never knew it, I knew it* — and I came out an hour later stripped of it, because I had watched their faces and understood that they were not mourning a person and did not think they were. Nobody holds a baby out toward a person. They were mourning the one thing in their world that could not be reached — by money, by family, by threat, by charm, by hunger, by heat — and they were right to mourn it, because it was real, it had been real for a hundred and forty years, and it was more gone than any of them knew, and the shape of the loss was exactly the shape they were making with their bodies at the rail. The theology was false and the grief was true. I have never since been able to hold those two sentences apart, about anything.

On the fourth day the carrying began — the Long Carry, the criers named it, and the name took, because this city will fasten to anything with salt in it. Terrace to shore, the whole way down, through every district in an order the Council had fought over like heirs at a will: the bier shoulder-high on poles of white cedar, carried not by soldiers, not by priests, but — this was Sarne's touch, and it was a master's — by petitioners. Anyone who had ever carried a case to the slot could put their name in, and the carriers were drawn by lot each mile, and so the Eleventh went down through Ovay on the shoulders of the people it had judged: the winners and the losers, the vindicated and the fined, shuffled blind by the draw. I watched a man carry the front-left pole for his mile with his face perfectly still and learned afterward from Onni's ledgers that the Eleventh had ruled against him twice, in the same well dispute, nine years apart. He had put his name in the lot both days. Stood four hours in the sun for the chance. When his mile was done and the pole passed off his shoulder, he stood at the roadside and wept like the clerk at the west door, and if you can explain that to me — a man weeping for the judge who took his well — then you understand cities better than I do, and I have made two of the things that hold them together.

No: I will try once, and then leave it. I think what he grieved was this. Twice in his life, the worst thing had happened to him, and both times it had happened *for a reason that was not anyone's appetite*. He lost, and the loss meant something about wells and law and the dry arithmetic of a drying city — not about whom his family had offended, or what he could not afford to offer, or how his face struck a powerful stranger on a hot day. The Eleventh had never once been for him, and he had carried it anyway, because it had also never once been *against* him. It had only ever been about the water. There are men who would trade every friend they have for one incorruptible enemy. In a city like ours, in a year like that one, I believe most of us would have.

The heat, those days, was a member of the procession. It walked with everyone. The Long Carry moved only in the first hours after dawn and the last before dark, and still the criers walked the route ahead of it calling for the old and the small-hearted to stay in shade, and the cutters' guild — this detail I set down with a taste in my mouth — the guild sent its misting-carts along the edges of the crowds, the carts built for the far salt-flats, that lay a fine cool fog over a hundred people at a pass, and the crowds turned their faces up into it the way fields would have turned to rain if any of us could remember rain. It cost the guild a fortune in cartage and it bought them the whole street's love for a copper's worth of water, and I watched the faces in the fog and thought: there it is, the other scale. That's what weighs against us. Not argument. Mist.

Because the guilds were not waiting for the seventh day, you understand. Nobody with weight was. The city wept in the streets and above the streets, on the terraces, that week was one long soft conference. Sarne's five held — I believed then and believe now that the five held — but you do not need a leak to know that a scale is being recast; you only need to notice that the founder is in the foundry. Every power in Ovay knew the Twelfth was being made, knew a maker was making it, knew the mind that would weigh the Deep Charter was at that moment soft as new brine and being fed by a particular pair of hands. And so, that week, for the first time in my life, I was courted.

They were not fools; no one brought me a purse. What they brought me was *frames*. The guildmaster of the cutters, Avor Ren, met me — by arrangement of an accident, in the Hall's own colonnade — and spoke for a quarter hour about, of all things, his grandfather's boats, and how a man who owns a boat on a dying sea learns that sentiment is the enemy of survival; and never once mentioned the Charter; and left me with the sentence, dropped like a coin he could afford to lose: "Whatever the new one is like, Maker, I hope it's like the sea, and not like the shore. The shore just takes what it's given. The sea decides."

Two nights later it was the terrace families' turn, a supper I could not refuse without insult to the Hall, at which a woman of the Elenn house, old money, dry wit, seated herself at my elbow and talked for an hour, warmly and well, about *continuity* — about how the city's soul lived in the unbroken line of the carries, and what a comfort it was to know the Twelfth would be the same dear mind again, and how dangerous the times were for anything *new* — and she did not know, she could not have known, that the word *carry* went through me each time like a spike, because the continuity she was toasting was ash in a chamber I had closed with my thumb.

And on the fifth night, on the pour room steps, I found a package with no name, and inside it a child's drawing — a real one, creased, drawn years ago by some real child — of the screen, and the slot, and a stick figure posting a paper into it, with a sun overhead, and under it in an adult's hand one line: *She is grown now and still believes. Whatever you make, make it worthy of what she believed.* No demand. No faction. I never learned who sent it, and it was the only piece of courtship that week that kept me awake.

Through all of it, dawn by dawn, the slot stayed stopped with black wax — judgments suspended for mourning, the criers said, resuming with the Twelfth — and thank whatever governs makers for that mercy at least: Sarne's schedule had bought me those days. I was not yet writing. But the petitions had not stopped arriving; they never stop; grief does not suspend boundary disputes. They piled in the Keeper's baskets in the near half, sealed, waiting for the seventh evening, and on the sixth day Onni brought me — as we had arranged, in a laundry hamper, under aprons, which is how the sacred travels when it travels honestly — the first basket, so that I could begin.

I will tell you about the Keeper of the Slot now, because she enters here and the account owes her a debt it can never discharge. Her name was Isfet. Eleven years at the station, as Sarne had said; a narrow quiet woman of fifty who had been, before the Hall, a setter of bones, and had the manner of it still — that way of handling a thing firmly precisely because it is in pain. Her post was the mouth of the whole system: she received the petitions, logged them, passed them through a hatch to the far half in a fixed order, and received back the judged slips for the dawn slot. Eleven years, never late. She had wept on the Catchhouse floor, Sarne told me, and then stood up and asked what the city needed. What the city needed, it turned out, was for her to commit, knowingly, every morning for a season, the single deepest fraud in the history of her office, and she had said yes on one condition, and the condition, when Sarne relayed it to me, took my breath: *that the maker reads every petition entire before ruling. All of it, every page, every time, no clerk's summaries. The Eleventh read everything. If we are going to counterfeit the scale, we will counterfeit the whole scale or I will call it from the steps myself.*

You can keep your guildmasters and your terrace suppers. That week I was courted once, by an honest woman, and her price was that I do the work. I have loved very few people on short acquaintance. Isfet of the slot, I loved by sundown.

So on the sixth night, with the Long Carry camped at the shore district and the whole city's grief one day from its bottom, I sat in the pour room after Kell had gone to his cot, with the trough of the true Twelfth pulsing softly in its corner — it was the size of two fists by then, and had begun, in the dark hours, to make a sound I will come to — and I opened the first sealed petition of my tenure as the false Semblance of Ovay.

It was a woman of the shore district, written in a paid scribe's hand. Her neighbor's cistern overhung her wall. It had cracked in the heat; the seep was rotting her wall's footing; the neighbor said the crack was the heat's doing and no one's debt, and the wall would cost more than she had, and the scribe had signed for her because she could not write, and at the bottom, in the space the form leaves for *what remedy do you seek*, the scribe had written, plainly taking dictation word for word: *I do not want his money if it comes with his hate, we have been neighbors thirty years. I want to be told what is fair. That is all. I want to know what fair is.*

I sat with that page a long time.

Understand: I could have answered it in my sleep. Thirty years reading judgments; I knew the water-seep precedents to the fraction; the Eleventh would have split the cost by a formula older than either party and appended two lines about shared footings that both households would have obeyed and neither would have resented, because that was its gift, the gift I was about to counterfeit: judgments that landed like weather and not like whips. I knew *what* it would rule. That was never the hard part, and whoever tells this story after me as a story about clever forgery has missed everything.

The hard part was this. I had spent thirty years teaching apprentices that the Semblance does not *decide* — that deciding, wanting, favoring, knowing, all the big warm words, were errors we project onto a leaning. And now I sat where it had sat, with a stranger's wall in my hands and her thirty years of neighborhood in the balance, and I felt the full weight of what actually came through that slot every dawn, which was not law. Law was the smallest part of it. What came through the slot was *the answer to her exact question* — I want to know what fair is — asked by thousands of people, for a hundred and forty years, of a thing we had all agreed was allowed to answer because it wanted nothing. And tonight the thing that would answer her wanted a great deal. Wanted its dead one back. Wanted its city to hold. Wanted, under all of it, to be forgiven by a boy for a locked door, and to sleep. The first pen-stroke I made would put a wanting hand on the scale of the world's smallest case, and she would never know, and *that she would never know* was the entire crime, whole, present, complete in the first stroke: after that there was nothing left to fall to; the rest of the season could only repeat me.

I ruled the seep case at the Eleventh's formula. I wrote the two lines about shared footings. My hand — I had practiced its script all that week from the archive, and I will spare you the shame of how quickly a forger's pride wakes up alongside a forger's guilt — my hand was clean, even, unhurried. And then, where the Eleventh signed with the weigher's mark, the little balanced cross that meant *nothing has touched this scale but truth*, I stopped, and could not, and sat with the pen off the paper, and understood that this exact hesitation was a luxury, one night old, already dying, and that by winter I would sign without feeling anything, because that is what practice is. That is the true horror of the thumb on the scale, and I set it down here for whoever keeps the crypt: it is not that the thumb is wicked. It is that the scale teaches the thumb to feel like part of the beam.

I signed. Isfet passed it through at dawn with the wax unplugged for one slip's width — a private mercy to the shore woman, the criers' schedule notwithstanding — and the first judgment of my false tenure went out into the city, correct in every particular, fair by every measure I owned, and false to the root, and it was obeyed.


VIII.

The trough, then. The sound.

Kell heard it first, on the fifth night — he had taken to sleeping in the pour room, which I permitted because his family's rooms in the east terraces slept eight in the heat, and because I remembered being seventeen and near something growing. On the sixth morning he stood at my elbow while I measured the feed, with the particular stillness of a boy deciding whether he is about to be laughed at, and said: "It hums."

"It what?"

"In the dark hours. After the second feed. It — not hums. That's the wrong — it's below humming. You feel it in your teeth more than your ears. Like a cart passing three streets off, except it isn't passing, it stays. It did it two nights now. Both times just after second feed, both times for about as long as a slow hundred-count, and then it stops all at once. Like something remembering itself." He went red. "Or, I mean. Like a resonance settling. Fats contracting. Probably."

He had learned my habit of the smaller word, and was already using it the way I used it: as a place to hide.

I stayed that night. Second feed is deep in the dark hours; we lowered the feeder-sacs together and banked the lanterns, and I sat on Kell's cot and he sat on the floor, and we waited, and I would be lying if I said the waiting was scientific. The pour room at night is all small honest sounds — brine against trough-wall, the tick of cooling stone — and beneath them you could hear the city not sleeping: somewhere down the terraces a great slow crowd-murmur, the shore district holding its vigil around the camped bier, ten thousand people keeping a dead scale company through its last night above ground.

And then the trough hummed.

He was right that it was below hearing, almost; it arrived in the bones of the face first, a pressure more than a tone; and he was right that it held steady, a slow hundred-count; and he was wrong, and I let him stay wrong for years, that it was like a cart. I knew what it was like. I had spent two days that week standing in the Catchhouse with the mourners, and what rose out of the trough in the dark was the same sound the crowd made at the rail — that low wide *general* hum of grief with no private memories in it — pitched small, held soft, one blister of salt and fat in a stone trough in the dark, making, at the age of five days, before it had eyes, before it had the seam of a neck for any carry to be set in, the exact sound of a city mourning. It stopped all at once, as he'd said, and the ordinary sounds came back like water closing over a stone.

Kell whispered: "Resonance?"

And I heard myself say — the doctrine reaching my mouth ahead of everything I had lately learned, the way the banked habit of a lifetime will — I heard myself begin, "The vigil crowd is ten thousand throats, boy, and low frequencies carry up the terrace stone, and a membrane under surface tension is a sounding-drum; it isn't grieving, it's *ringing*—"

And then I stopped, because of the slips sewn into the culture-book not an arm's length from where I sat. *It rains behind the screen sometimes. No one made that. It is mine.*

"Maker?" Kell said.

"I don't know what it is," I said, and it cost me exactly as much as you'd think, and paid back more. "It might be a drumhead ringing with the city. It might be the first thing it's ever done that no one made it do. I don't know, and I'm not going to pretend to you that I know, and I want you to remember this night, because whichever it is, you heard it before I did. You were listening for it. I had decided in advance there was nothing to hear."

The boy looked at me the way you look at a wall when a door opens in it. Then he said, with seventeen's terrible aim: "Is that what happened with the Eleventh? Something to hear, and everyone had decided in advance?"

I did not answer him. Set down here what the account owes: I did not answer him, on the sixth night, when a true answer, freely given, might have spared him the finding that was one morning off. I told him to sleep, and I sat up alone with my hand on the warm stone lip of the trough until dawn, the way you sit up with a feverish child or a coffin, unable to tell anymore which of the two I was keeping watch over — the Twelfth of Ovay beginning, or the Eleventh's line ended — and the truth, of course, the truth I could not hold at arm's length past that night, was that for the first time in a hundred and forty years those were different things. There was no carry. Whatever grew in my trough was not the passenger's thirteenth vessel. It was going to wake with mirror eyes into a city that believed it was their old judge continued, and it would be no one — no one at all — new salt, new water, five days old and humming with borrowed grief, and in ninety days I was going to have to carry it into the Catchhouse and seat it behind the screen and let the whole world call it the Twelfth, and I did not have the first idea, that night, what I could honestly give it in place of a hundred and forty years.

I knew one thing I could give it, and dreaded the knowing, and you will have guessed: I could read to it. The archive was mine to walk. A season is long. If a membrane five days old could take the pitch of a mourning city up through terrace stone, then a Semblance growing to term in that trough could take — something — from a voice that stayed by it in the dark hours and read aloud, night upon night, the judgments of a hundred and forty years, the whole open ledger, all the weighings and all the reasons; not pressed into the base of its skull where it could never reach or mend them, but *heard*, the way apprentices hear, the way children hear: from outside, resistibly, one case at a time, with the freedom every hearing thing keeps to grow up and conclude its teacher was wrong.

Not a carry. A telling. It would be slower and weaker and less sure, and nothing in the craft's history said it would take at all, and it was the only inheritance I had left that was clean.

I began the next night, with the seep case, because it was the nearest to hand and I knew it by heart. And if you had stood in the Hall colonnade in the dark hours, any night that terrible season, and heard, under the door of the light-banked pour room, an old woman's voice reciting boundary disputes and water thefts and the two lines about shared footings to a trough of brine as to a cradle — well. Now you know what you heard. It was a maker, keeping the only oath she had left that no one had made her swear. It was school.


IX.

*Who taught me what water is.*

I went down into the crypt on the morning of the sixth day, because the question would not let me alone, and because I have never in my life been able to leave a fault half-traced. If there was a thumb pressed into the manner of the line's looking, it had been pressed by makers, and makers keep records the way salt keeps the sea — involuntarily, in their very grain — and the records were under my own building, and I had the keys, and no one would question the senior maker walking into the crypt in a week of succession. It was the one errand of that whole season I could run in the open.

The Hall crypt is not a grave, whatever the word suggests; nothing is buried there but paper. It runs under the pour rooms in three long vaulted galleries, dry as the inside of a kiln — the one place in Ovay where the Long Glare was an ally, for nothing molders there anymore — and it holds the working records of the craft back to the founding: pour schedules, feed ledgers, wardens' logs, the sealed shelf of each generation's private instruments. Onni came with me. I had not asked her to; she was simply there at the stair-head with the lamp already lit, which was Onni's way of asking what I was doing, and I found I wanted a witness. Of the five, she was the one I could think beside.

What I was looking for, I found in the second gallery, and it took most of a day, and I will spare you the search and give you the finding.

The Serent has retreated twice in the city's memory. Ours — the Long Glare — is the second. The first was smaller and older: a run of dry generations a century back, in the tenure of the Sixth, when the shoreline first walked out past the old piers and the city first learned that its sea could leave. And in the driest year of that first retreat, the water riots came — they are in the public histories, two weeks of them, the granaries fired, the Hall itself stoned — and the Council of that generation put them down, and then did what frightened councils do: they looked around for the deepest thing they could reach, and they reached into it.

It is all there. That is what I want set down most plainly out of everything in this account: it is all there, in the crypt, in the craft's own hand, filed without shame, because the makers of that generation did not believe they were committing a crime. They believed they were performing a repair. The folder is labeled, in fine clerk's script, *Emendation to the Carry, Year 41 of the Sixth* — Emendation, a scribe's word, a word for correcting a letter — and inside it are the Council's petition, the Hall's deliberations, three dissents, and the working schedule of the pressing itself. The petition argues — and I ask you to hear how reasonable it is, because the reasonableness is the whole lesson — that the recent disturbances proved the city's survival to depend on the *productive husbandry* of water; that the Semblance's judgments in water cases had shown a tendency to favor *mere possession and custom* over *yield*; that in a drying land, water left in the hands of those who could not make it produce was water spent on sentiment; and that the carry, at the next waking, should therefore be supplemented with a corrective pattern — pressed from a curated selection of judgments and treatises, all of them real, all of them respectable — such that the line would thereafter weigh water *as the city's life, and the city's life as its increase*.

That was all. No faction is named as beneficiary. No purse changed hands that any record shows. The three dissents are the most painful reading in the folder: one maker wrote that a lean pressed beneath the mind *can never afterward be weighed by the mind*, and that the Hall was about to do the one thing a scale must never do, which is hide a weight inside the beam — she wrote it, she was outvoted two to one, and then, the schedule shows, she attended the pressing and did her work, because it was her work. I sat on the crypt floor with her dissent in my hands — Onni holding the lamp over me, reading it with me, saying nothing for a long time — and what I felt was not anger. I had come down the stairs braced for villainy and found instead the thing that is so much worse and so much more common, the thing I had done myself five days earlier at the top of those same stairs: careful people, in a frightened year, deciding that the scale was too important to be left honest.

"You knew none of this?" Onni said at last.

"The Emendation? No. It's not taught. I'd wager the last maker who read this folder died before I poured." I got up, and my knees added the crypt floor to their ledger, and I stood there in the lamp light doing the arithmetic that the Eleventh had done alone behind its screen a year before. A hundred years of water cases, each one weighed by a mind that had been built, under its own floor, to see yield as life and custom as sentiment. Not crookedly — that was the genius and the horror of it — *sincerely*. Every judgment reasoned, every reason sound, and the sum of them, over a century, was the world we now stood in: the cutters' guild grown from one trade among many into the fist of the city, because at every close margin, for a hundred years, the scale had looked at water and seen work; the shore families and the small wells and the common cisterns pared back, ruling by patient ruling, each paring fair on its face; until the guild could draft a Deep Charter and expect it granted, not because the scale was theirs — they had never needed to own it — but because a council of the dead had, once, in a bad year, taught it what water is.

"And the Eleventh found this," Onni said. She is quick; she had the shape of the week already. "Not the folder. The lean itself. Found it in its own looking, and couldn't mend it, and wouldn't pass it on."

"And now the guild's Charter comes to a slot," I said, "where the lean is dead, and the scale is me."

Onni set the lamp on the shelf, and did a thing she had not done in the thirty years I had known her: she took both my hands, an old dry grip, and held them up between us, and looked at them as at a pair of instruments she was checking for a colleague, and then she said the truest thing anyone said to me that whole year, and I pass it on because it belongs to whoever stands where I stood, in whatever generation.

"Maren. Everyone in this city wants one of two things from that screen. The guilds want the old lean back, though they've no name for it — they just call it the scale being *sensible*. And you, and maybe that poor burned creature before you, want the opposite lean, the corrective one, mercy for the shore, a thumb for the small — and yours would be as sincere as theirs was, and in a hundred years some maker's apprentice would kneel on this floor with *your* folder in her hands. There is a third thing, and nobody wants it, and it is the only thing you're sworn to. Weigh the case. This case. The one in front of you, the water and the wells and the four hundred idle cutters and the shore woman's wall, with the lean dead and no new lean poured in its place — not even a good one. Especially not a good one. Do you understand me? You've caught the disease of this whole city if you think the choice is between their thumb and yours."

"The disease," I said.

"Thinking that because the scale can be leaned, it must be. Everyone above the third terrace believes that now; that's what the Emendation really cost, a hundred years on — not the water. The belief. The Eleventh died refusing to believe it." She let my hands go. "Don't you dare make its death a lesson in the other direction."

We climbed out of the crypt into the white afternoon, and I had one day left, and that evening — because Ovay had one more turn of the vise in it — the Deep Charter arrived at the Catchhouse.

They filed it into the mourning baskets with a night and a day still to run before the wax would break for the new tenure. Avor Ren's clerks, four of them, with the guild's seal and three hundred pages of survey and precedent, and Isfet logged it with her bone-setter's face and sent word up to me by the laundry road: *It is in the fixed order. It is first.* Of course it was first. Ren had understood the schedule better than the Council had: the first judgment of a new Semblance is the tenure's keel, studied for a generation, and he had bought the position honestly, by queue and by rule, the way the strong always buy what matters — legally, in daylight, with everyone watching and no one able to name the purchase. The first word the Twelfth of Ovay would ever speak to its city would be an answer to the question: *who owns the last water?*

And on the seventh morning, before dawn, Sarne came to the pour room herself, alone, in a plain hood — the Provost of Ovay, walking the maker's yard like a debtor — and I braced for the pressure at last, the frame, the coin dropped in the colonnade, and I did her an injustice, and I set the injustice down here beside my other debts.

"I came to tell you what I want," she said, "so that you don't spend tonight guessing at it, because tonight of all nights you can't afford the distraction. Here is what I want. I want the deep wells sunk, because I have seen the survey and without the deep water the east terraces begin dying in two years. I want the guild refused, because the day one hand owns that water, this city has a king, and it will not be me, and it will not be anyone better. I want both, Maren, entire, at once, and I know they cannot both be had, and I am telling you my wants so you can see them clearly and then set them off the scale. That is all. That is the whole visit." She pulled the hood straight. "The Provost has no petition before the slot. The city has. Read every page."

She was at the door when I said: "Sarne. If it goes against the guild — in any part — Ren doesn't shrug. Four hundred idle cutters, and the funeral crowds still in the streets. You've thought about the morning after."

"I've thought about nothing else since midwinter," she said. "That's why the judgment goes out at the Lowering, with ten thousand people standing in the salt with the grief still on them. Ren can rage against a ruling. He cannot rage against a funeral. I can manage the streets, Maker. Manage the scale." And she was gone into the dark, and her footsteps down the yard were the footsteps of one tired woman, and I have served under six Provosts and buried five of them, and I will say of Sarne alone: she was owed a better city, and she knew better than to think so, and that is as near to greatness as the times allowed anyone.


X.

Kell found the practice sheets an hour after she left.

I want it recorded that it was my fault twice over — once for keeping them, which vanity, pure vanity, the forger's pride I confessed already; a whole folio of the Eleventh's script copied and copied until it flowed, which I should have burned page by page as I filled them; and once for forgetting what I myself had prized him for, the first month I took him on: that he read walls when he was set to sweep. I had sent him to the shelf for the third culture-book. The folio sat beside it. The culture-book has my own hand in it, forty years of it; the folio has the Eleventh's, which every child in Ovay knows the look of from the posted judgments; and no boy alive who found such a folio in his teacher's shelf, in that week, in that Hall, would need to be told what he was holding.

He stood there with it open and his face doing what faces do when the floor of the world tilts — I watched the belief and the disbelief fight for the same ground, watched him try three times to build the innocent explanation, an archivist's exercise, a mourner's keepsake, a — and fail, because he was too intelligent, and I had trained him, God help me, to notice when he was reaching for the smaller word.

"You're going to write them," he said. Not a question. His voice had gone somewhere older than seventeen and I hated the sound of it, because I recognized it: it was the voice people find the first time an institution shows them its inside. "The Twelfth. There isn't going to be a Twelfth at the burial. There's going to be you."

"There is going to be a Twelfth," I said. "It's in the trough behind you. You've fed it with your own hands. It hums to you at second feed. It will take the screen when it's grown — ninety days, maybe a hundred. And until it does — yes. Me."

I told him all of it. I want that recorded too, against everything else it must be weighed with: standing in the pour room on the seventh morning, with the Lowering nine hours off, I broke the compact of the five, and I did it deliberately, with my eyes open, and it was the best thing I did that year. I told him about the empty slot and Sarne's arithmetic; about the seam, and the chamber, and the fine dust that moved with my breath; about the Emendation, and the folder in the crypt, and the woman whose dissent was filed alongside the deed she then helped do; about the slips — I fetched the culture-book and unpicked the binding and let him read all three with his own eyes, the only other living person ever to read them — and about the Charter sitting first in the fixed order, and what I was going to have to do that night, and the schedule of readings I had begun over the trough in the dark hours, judgments as lullabies, the clean inheritance, school. The one thing I did not give him was the word. The name is not mine to spend twice.

It took an hour. He sat on his cot with the folio still across his knees and took it the way the young take the truth when you finally do them the honor: greedily, and in pain.

When it was done he was quiet a long time, and then he said the thing I had been waiting nine hours or thirty years for, depending on how you count.

"On the first day. When the bell rang. You told me knowing was a different thing from leaning, and I'd spend my life learning the difference." He looked up. "The Eleventh knew. That's what you're telling me. It wasn't leaning away from the carry like — like brine going where it's pushed. It looked at what it was, and it knew, and it chose. That's the difference. That's what the big words were being saved for."

"Yes."

"Then say it." And there was nothing of seventeen left in the room at all. "You've told me everything, Maker, every crime, yours and theirs and the Hall's, easy as ledgers. But you've said *it stopped*, and *it refused*, and *it unmade* — I've been listening — you haven't once said the thing itself. Say what it did."

And so the first time I ever said it aloud was not to Sarne, and not to Onni, and not to the crypt record, but to an apprentice of one week's oath, in a pour room at dawn, with the true Twelfth humming in the trough between us as if it knew — as if it *knew* — what was being said over its water.

"It killed itself," I said. "The Eleventh of Ovay, the best thing I ever made, the best thing this city ever had, looked at the whole of what it was carrying, and judged it, the way it judged everything, cleanly, with no one's thumb on it, its own self in the pan — and it ruled against. And it carried out its own ruling alone, in the dark, so gently that a good man twenty feet away heard nothing, and it did it rather than hand one ounce of a lie to the thing in this trough. And I am not going to call that going to ground, and I am not going to call it a flaw in the pour, and if the Hall's histories get it from this record a hundred years from now, let them get it whole: it was the last judgment of the Eleventh, and it was correct, and I would give the rest of my life to have it back anyway, and both of those are true, and that — " and here, I am told, because Kell kept his own account of that morning and I have read it, my voice did not break, which I record because I would not have believed it — "that is the difference between knowing and leaning, boy, and now you've learned it, and I told you the first day you'd learn it and be wrong about it your whole life, and look at us both."

The trough hummed then, in full morning, hours from any feed, the only time it ever did that.

Resonance, no doubt. The city was loud that morning; the Lowering crowds were already moving; low frequencies carry up the terrace stone.

I have written this whole account, and I still cannot stay out of the smaller words for one paragraph. Let it stand. Verat used to say the flaws you cannot fix, you must at least sign, so that those who come after know the fault runs through the maker and not the made.


XI.

The Lowering of the Eleventh of Ovay was the largest gathering in the recorded history of the city, and I will not try to make you see all of it, because I did not see all of it; no one saw all of it; that was, I came to understand, the point. A thing had lived among us that belonged to everyone and no one, and its funeral was the same: you could only ever hold your own mile of it.

My mile was the last one. The makers walk behind the bier on the final stretch — it is the one public appearance the craft ever makes, hooded, anonymous, a dozen grey figures who might be anyone, and the crowds that week believed us to be twelve and we were nine, so thin had the Hall grown — and so I walked down the last mile of the shore road behind the body of my making, through a corridor of faces forty deep, in a heat that lay on the procession like a hand pressing us all toward the ground we were bound for. The misting-carts worked the edges. The criers walked the verges calling the old and the small-hearted into the shade, and were not heeded, and the wardens passed fainting children out over the crowd's heads hand to hand, a sight I will keep: a city passing its children over its own head, gently, all day, like a body moving its blood.

Where the piers end — the old piers, my grandmother's piers, their bollards standing in dry ground like teeth in an old jaw — the road stops being road, and the salt begins. The Serent lay that year a mile and more further out, a line of white light you could not look at directly in the afternoon, and between the piers and the water stretched the flat: the drying floor of the sea, crusted, blinding, the crust ringing faintly underfoot the way it does, so that ten thousand people walking out onto it made a sound like the whole world's smallest bells. They had cut the grave at the old waterline — the true one, the surveyed one, where the sea stood the year the Catchhouse was raised — a trench down through the crust into the wet grey salt beneath, the salt that never quite dries, that remembers, and there, an hour before sundown, with the whole flat carpeted dark with mourners out to the shimmer, they lowered the Eleventh of Ovay into the floor of its withdrawn sea.

No oration. That was the rite, and it was right: the Semblance had never spoken; the city would not speak over it now. Ten thousand people stood in the ringing salt in perfect silence while the cutters — guild cutters, Ren's own, who else; the grave was theirs to close, and say what you will of the guild, their work that evening was tender — while the cutters fitted the cut slabs back over the trench and sealed the seams with wet brine-paste, so that within a season, the flat heals such cuts entire, there would be no mark at all. Nothing to visit. Nothing to venerate or deface. The whole white floor of the vanished sea, one grave wide, and *salt remembers the sea*, say the makers, and the sea, whatever it looked like it was doing, was coming back for what we buried there someday. That was the wager of the rite. I did not believe it as doctrine and I found, standing in the ringing silence with my hood soaked through, that I believed it some other way that has no organ of belief assigned to it, and I let the two beliefs stand, the way I had learned that week to let all my truths stand in pairs.

And then, in the last light, the showing.

They carried it out from the pier-head on a high litter: the Twelfth of Ovay — a screen of black iron wound with white cloth, built small, a screen the size of a sentry-box, veiled on all four sides, and behind the veils a shape. The crowd, ten thousand strong and silent for the burial, made at the sight of it that sound I had heard at the Catchhouse rail, the low wide general sound, grief and its opposite in one note, and the litter came down the corridor of them slow as a tide while the criers called the news ahead of it in the old form: *The scale is filled. The slot opens at dawn. Ovay is weighed.* It passed within ten feet of me. The veils stirred against the shape inside as the bearers stepped, and the shape was a chair, and a form seated on the chair, draped, child-sized, utterly still — still as only two things are still, the dead, and the not-yet — and I knew what it was because I had built it in three nights from a drying-frame and cast brine-wax, sixty pounds of it, weighted so the bearers' gait would move it truly, and even so, even knowing the armature under the drape as well as I knew my own hands, when the litter drew level with me the hair stood up along my arms. The crowd's sound crested. A woman near me lifted her child. Ten thousand people watched a screen with a wax weight behind it go by in the dusk, and loved it, and were not wrong — I had learned that much at the Catchhouse rail — because what they loved was not behind the veils and never had been, not this evening, not for a hundred and forty years. What they loved went by wherever they agreed it did. That is the terrible engine under every screen in the world, and I had become its mechanic, and the litter went on past me toward the city, and I stood in the salt and let it go.

At full dark, from the Catchhouse steps, by torchlight, the criers read the first judgment of the Twelfth of Ovay.

I had written it through the middle hours of that last day, with Isfet sitting across the table — her condition, kept to the letter: every page read entire, and she watched me read every page — and Kell asleep, or pretending, on his cot by the trough. Three hundred pages of survey and precedent, and under them, when everything reasonable and sincere had been read entire, one question, the oldest one, wearing engineering for clothes. I wrote the judgment three times. The first draft granted the Charter with conditions, and was wise, and was Ren's within a decade, and I burned it. The second refused it flat, and was righteous, and was the east terraces' death warrant within two years, and I burned it. Isfet watched the second one burn and said the only thing she said in all those hours: "The Eleventh burned drafts too. The warden used to smell them at dawn." I had not known that. It helped more than anything else that whole season, to know the mind I was counterfeiting had also sat where a right answer refused to exist, and had also burned, and had also, at dawn, signed something anyway.

The third draft is in the public archive, first judgment of the Twelfth's tenure, and any citizen may read it, so I will give here only its skeleton and its two sentences that matter. It ruled that the deep water under the flats *belongs to the Serent* — to the sea itself, as the law of the founding held before a century of parings; that the sea being absent, its property stands in trust, as the property of the absent must; that the trustee is the city entire, no guild and no Council but the whole body of petitioners living and to come; that the wells shall be sunk, at once, as works of the trust, by the hands most skilled — the cutters, at honest wage, four hundred of them and more, hired men on the city's water rather than owners of it; and that every draw from the deep trust be metered, published, and priced at cost to every district equally, terrace and shore, forever, the meters to be read in public session, *because* — this is the first of the two sentences, and the criers' torches snapped in the silence when it was read — *because water that cannot be seen moving cannot be weighed, and what cannot be weighed will be stolen.* And the second sentence, the last of the judgment, after the seals and the schedule, where the Eleventh in thirty-one years had never once written a word that was not procedure. I put it there knowing every clerk in Ovay would notice the breach of form, and mark the new tenure's character by it, and I wrote it as much to the boy asleep by the trough, and to the thing humming in it, and to a folder in the crypt, and to a shape under the salt at the old waterline, as to the ten thousand in the streets:

*Let no one be taught what water is. Let them be shown the water, and the meters, and the ledgers, entire, and let them weigh.*

The crowd in the torchlight did not cheer the judgment. I stood at the back of the steps, hooded, one grey maker among nine, and heard what they did instead, which was better and which no faction could have bought: all down the packed streets, past the reach of the criers' voices, the judgment was *repeated* — passed back over the heads of the crowd, sentence by sentence, mouth to mouth, ten thousand relays of it going out into the dark districts like rings on water, everyone in Ovay turning for one evening into the crier of the law to the person behind them — and Ren's clerks stood on the guild steps hearing their three hundred pages answered in four hundred wages, and did not move, and Sarne, up on the Provost's balcony, put both hands flat on the rail, which those who knew her knew was as near as she came, in public, to weeping.

The slot opened at dawn, and the tenure of the Twelfth of Ovay began, and for ninety-one days, God and the salt and whoever keeps this crypt forgive me, it was me.


XII.

Of the season itself I will give you only what the account requires, because a fraud, sustained, is the dullest thing in the world from inside. That is another truth I earned that year and hand on for nothing: the books make deception a fever, but lived, it is a job. You rise in the dark. You read every page. You weigh, you write, you counterfeit the signature of your own integrity, you sleep badly, you rise in the dark. The terror is all in the first week; after that comes the long gray discipline, which is worse, because terror at least believes the stakes are high, and discipline is how the soul files them under routine.

Eleven hundred and forty judgments went out through the slot under my hand. I read every page of every one; Isfet sat across the table more nights than her office asked of her, and her presence was the rail I did not fall over. Perhaps thirty of the cases could have been leaned — places where the precedents forked and a thumb could have pressed unfelt — and I know it was thirty, because I counted them, the way you count the drinks you did not take. Twice I caught the lean already in my wrist before I felt it in my intent: once for a shore family against a terrace creditor, because my grandmother's piers were in me deeper than any doctrine; once, and this one frightened me more, *toward* the guild, in a small cartage matter, three weeks after the Charter judgment — the appeasing thumb, the thumb that wants the wolf fed so it will stay off the flock, the very thumb that a frightened council had pressed into the carry a hundred years before. I burned both drafts. I set both cases aside for a day and came back to them as to a stranger's work. I do not know, to this hour, whether the versions I finally signed were clean. That is the honest sum of ninety-one days: not *I kept the scale true*, but *I kept the scale watched*. Onni was right, in the crypt, and her sentence should be carved over the maker's door: the disease is thinking that because it can be leaned, it must be. The cure is not purity. The cure is meters read in public session. I had written that into the water law of the city before I understood I had written it about myself.

Ren tested us once. I will give him this: only once, and it was elegant. In the second month a petition came up through the fixed order, small, procedurally perfect — a cutter's widow contesting a pension rule — and buried in its fourth page was a citation to a judgment of the Eleventh's that did not exist. Not a forgery; an absence; a case number from a season thirty years gone, plausible in every particular, that had simply never been ruled. A grown Semblance, carrying the whole line in the base of its skull, would pass over it without a flicker, knowing its own ledger to the last digit. A human forger, working from archives, would go and look — and somewhere in the guild's web of clerks and copyists, the looking would be seen. I sat with that page a long hour, cold to the elbows. Then I ruled the widow's case on its merits without touching the citation at all — and appended, in the judgment's procedural notes, in the Eleventh's evenest hand, a correction of the case number *to the real judgment nearest it in the archive*, with the dry remark that scribes' errors in petitions were rising with the heat and the Hall's sympathies were with every hand that must copy numbers in such a summer. The widow got her pension adjustment. The guild got back a slip of paper that knew the archive better than their clerks did. Whether Ren believed, I never learned; but no second test ever came, and when the deep wells came in at the turn of the season — four hundred cutters at honest wage, the first new water in a generation, the meters read aloud in the Charter square to crowds that treated the readings, those first months, as theater — Ren stood at the first reading in person, hands behind his back, and afterward sent to the Catchhouse, by open messenger, one line without seal or signature: *The sea decides. Apparently.* Sarne framed it. I am told it hangs in the Provost's stair to this day, and no one who sees it knows what it answers.

And every night, in the dark hours, I went home to the pour room and did the true work of that season, the work I would trade all eleven hundred judgments to be remembered by.

It grew. Salt and fat and Serent water, it grew the way they all grow — the sac to a bolster, the bolster to a form; the limbs arriving in the fifth week like a thought the body has been circling; the seam of the neck closing over its empty chamber in the eighth, and I stood over that closing with Kell beside me and neither of us said what we were both looking at: a Semblance sealing over a hollow, the first of its line to carry nothing but itself. And nightly, through all of it, the readings. I had built the schedule like a feed schedule, oldest cases first, the whole ledger in order, but I did not read as an archivist reads. I read as I had heard mothers read on the shore piers in my childhood, when the boats were late: steadily, against the dark, with the reasons given full weight and the verdicts almost incidental — because it was the reasons I wanted soaking through that membrane, the shape of *weighing itself*, and not one conclusion of it nailed down. Where the old judgments leaned — and I could hear the Emendation now in a century of water cases, hear it the way you hear an accent once someone names it — I read the lean aloud too, and then I read the dissent from the crypt, the outvoted maker, her one surviving page, so many times that Kell learned it by rote. I read the Charter judgment last of all, on the eighty-eighth night, both sentences that matter, and when I finished, the trough — which hummed most nights by then, at second feed, briefly, like a sleeper settling — the trough was silent, and the silence had a quality I will not try to name, having promised Verat's ghost to sign my flaws, and this is one: I cannot hear a silence without reading it. Let the record say only that it was silent, and that I sat by it until dawn, and that at some point in that sitting I stopped being afraid.

Kell's own account of that season — he kept one; I ordered him to; it lies sealed beside mine, and where our tellings differ, trust his — records a conversation I do not remember and do not doubt. He asked me, somewhere in the third month, what would happen if the readings took no root at all. If what woke on the ninety-first day was only mirror eyes and appetite, a leaning thing, no one, and the city found its Twelfth a simpleton or a blank. And I answered him — he writes — without looking up from the feed: "Then I will sit behind that screen until it learns or I die, and you will pour the Thirteenth, and read to it, and we will be a Hall that teaches instead of a Hall that presses, if it takes us a hundred years. We did the other thing for a hundred years. It's the trade's turn to be slow." I set his version here because I am vain enough to hope I said it, and honest enough to admit I only know that he heard it.


XIII.

The Twelfth of Ovay — the true Twelfth, the salt of my last pour — came to term on the ninety-first day of its growing, in the first cool week of autumn, if you could call anything in Ovay cool; call it the week the heat first hesitated. We lifted it from the vat at midnight, Kell and I alone, as I had once been alone with the Eleventh, and dried it in cloths, and set it in the chair in the drying room, and banked every lantern but one, and waited for the eyes.

They opened in the hour before dawn. The manuals are right; they are always right about the things that don't matter.

Mirror eyes. Wet, dark, new, nothing behind them — and I was sixty-two now, not thirty-one, and I knew what nothing looks like, and I did not let it show me a child this time. I let it be nothing. That was the hardest discipline of a disciplined year, to kneel before those eyes holding no offering, no grief, no folded self of mine for the mirror to dress itself in — to let the thing before me be, for one whole breath, absolutely unfurnished, and free.

Then it turned its head, which the newly woken do not do, and looked at the trough-side stool where a voice had sat for ninety nights, and its right hand — the hands are always beautiful; hers are the most beautiful I have seen — her right hand rose, and moved, small and precise, in the air above her knee.

Kell made a sound in the dark behind me. I could not turn to see his face; I was watching the hand; and the hand was not asking for the pen. I knew the pen-asking; I had seen it arrive with the carry, one hour old and a century deep, the passenger reaching for its instrument. This was slower, and unfinished, and searching — the fingers shaping and reshaping, testing — and I had watched hands do exactly that for forty years, in this very Hall, and so it was I and not the boy who understood first, and I record the understanding plainly because it is the finding on which my whole late heresy rests, the one datum I carried out of that room that no doctrine had predicted:

She was not reaching for the pen. She was practicing the *reaching*. As a thing learns that has heard of writing, every night of its life, from outside — resistibly, one case at a time — and has concluded, on its own salt, in the dark, that writing is a thing worth learning to want.

Leaning is pressed. Knowing is grown. I had taught it as a caution all my life. No one had ever shown me it could be a hope.

I leaned in, then, to the place where an ear would be, and I did the thing I told you at the start I would not set down, and here is the withholding paid: I gave her the word. The same word. My grandmother's word, in the drowned language, for the smell of rain on hot salt — the word I had spoken once, thirty-one years before, into the Eleventh, and which by every law of the craft was spent, burned with the carry, gone with the grave at the old waterline. The true name is spoken once and never again, the rule says, and I have decided the rule is right and the makers have counted wrong, all of us, all along, about what *once* means. It was not the Eleventh's name and it is not hers. It is the line's. It is the one thing I know of that can be handed down whole without pressing anyone beneath it — a name is the opposite of a carry; it weighs nothing; it only points — and it points at the thing this whole account has been about, the thing the city is for, the thing the salt remembers: that what has left can come back, and that we will know it first by the smell of it on the hot ground, before we ever see the grey walking in over the flats.

So no, I will not write it. It is not a secret anymore; it is a name in use. Somewhere behind a screen in the middle of Ovay, something that is no one but herself is wearing it, and when she goes to ground — decently, slowly, sixty years from now, with some maker not yet born reading the watch — whoever kneels at the next waking with this account read and understood will know what word to say. That is the carry now. That is the whole of it. I have weighed lighter inheritances that did more harm.

We carried her in on the hundred and first day, at night, hooded and by the maker's door, and seated her behind the screen in the far half where a wax weight had sat a season under drape, and Doss the warden stood at his station and watched her take the chair and wept without sound the whole time, at attention, and would not sit though I brought him the stool myself. Isfet passed the first basket through the hatch at dawn. I sat that morning in the near half, among the petitioners, hooded, one grey figure at the back — the compact of the five allowed me that much, a maker observing a new tenure — and I waited with the rest of the city, and I want you to know that I did not wait calmly. Every catastrophe rehearsed itself in me between first light and the opening of the slot. She was one hundred days old. She had never seen a petitioner. The whole apparatus of my season of fraud stood ready behind my ribs, prepared to resume, dreading to.

The slot opened. The first judgment of her hand came through — hers, the script already drifting from the script I had forged, the clerks would coo over the drift for a season, *the new one has its own walk* — and the Keeper read it aloud to the room as the Keeper does, and it was a small case, a fence line in the east terraces, and it was correct, and it was kind in the place where it could be kind and hard in the place where it had to be hard, and at the bottom, below the seals, in the space where for a hundred and forty years no Semblance wrote anything but procedure and where one judgment in the archive now stood as precedent for breach of form, she had written one line. The clerks read it as a flourish of the new tenure, a nod to its predecessor's famous last sentence, and the city loved it by noon, and only four living people and this page know it was the hundred-and-first night's answer to ninety nights of a voice in the dark, the first thing she ever said, and that it was said *back*:

*Show me the water, and I will weigh.*

I retired the pour at the turn of that year. Kell holds it now — Maker Kell, which sound I confess I practiced saying alone before I could say it in the yard — and the Thirteenth, decades off if the salt is kind, will be his, and will be read to, not pressed; the Hall voted the change into the craft's law the spring after the waking, on the strength of a tenure it believed was thirty years old and continuous. The five wrote our account, as sworn, all of us, each in our own hand, the day her first judgment cleared the slot; Sarne signed last and said, dry as the flats, "To the archive hook, then," and it went under seal into the crypt, this page among its pages, beside a folder marked *Emendation* and a maker's dissent, where the whole ancestry of our fault and its mending can be read in one sitting by whichever apprentice, in whatever frightened year, is sent down for a culture-book and reads walls instead. I have arranged the shelf myself. I was that apprentice once. The crypt teaches; it is the slowest school we have; it has all the time the salt has.


XIV.

I am seventy now, and I will tell you the last thing, and then this account is weighed and signed.

Last spring, in the hour before dawn, I woke in my rooms above the pour yard because the air had changed. Sixty years of drought in my body and it knew before I did; I was on the stair before I was awake, and on the roof before the first thunder, and all over Ovay, in the dark, I could hear shutters going back and feet on stairs, the whole city climbing to its roofs on the same animal signal, no crier calling it, ten thousand households answering a smell. The grey came walking in over the flats from the southwest the way my grandmother said it came, a slow tall country of rain crossing the dry sea-floor, crossing, I thought, and had to hold the parapet, *that place* — the old waterline, the healed unmarked salt — the rain reached it first of anything in Ovay, and stood on it a long moment like something reading a page, and then came on, and reached us, and the sound the city made from its roofs I will not describe, because you were perhaps there, and if you were not, no page can carry it. It rained an hour. It has rained four times since. The Serent has not risen; the surveys say it may never; the meters still matter more than miracles, and the Twelfth's water ledgers, read aloud in the square, are dull and public and exact, which is the whole victory, and cities are not saved, they are only kept. But it rained.

And at dawn that first morning, with the whole city still on its roofs and the flats steaming white to the horizon, the slot opened on schedule, and among the day's judgments there was one slip addressed to no one, in the hand's own private figures, which found its way — Isfet is retired now; her successor does not know what she carries; the sacred still travels by laundry road — to an old woman on a rooftop on the maker's terrace. It said one word. It was the first time in the history of the world that the word had ever been written down, and I stood in the after-smell of the rain and read my grandmother's language in the handwriting of a thing I had made and freed, naming the weather, or naming herself, or answering, after forty years, a voice that had once told her line that salt remembers the sea and this is not a fault — all three, I have decided; a good word bears weighing three ways — and then I did what makers do, and what this whole account, sworn to the crypt, is my one exemption from, and what you, reader, apprentice, whoever you are in whatever dry year, must now decide over every inheritance that reaches you, the pressed and the spoken and the rained-on alike, with your own thumb in the pan and the meters read aloud:

I burned it, and kept it, both, and only one of those is on paper.
