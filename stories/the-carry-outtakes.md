# The Carry — outtakes

Orphaned alternate-draft material that previously sat after THE END in `the-carry.md`.
In this branch, Sarne proposes the forbidden *division* of the Eleventh instead of the
forged-judgments bargain, and the maker's examination finds a piece already taken from
the body — carried, by someone, to the habit-starter in her own pour room.

This is incompatible with the finished story (there, the carry is burned and *nothing*
is passed on — the empty chamber is the point), but the closing image of the doubled
starter is strong if a companion story ever wants it.

III.

"You can divide it," she said.

I had known she would say something like it and still the words went into me like a cold draft into that warm city. "No," I said.

"Maren."

"You don't say it to me and I don't say it to you. You send me back down and we find another way. There is no other way but you'll find one, because it's your job to find ways I can refuse."

"There isn't time for me to do my job the slow way." She leaned forward into a bar of light so that I could see all of her face, which was a thing she did on purpose, I understood later, spending herself so I'd spend myself. "I know what dividing is. I know it's forbidden. I've read the reason it's forbidden — I've read it three times since last night, in your own Hall's own hand — and I'll say it back to you so you know I'm not asking blind. You may not raise a Semblance from the substance of another because the child inherits the parent's carry. It doesn't come to the trough empty. It comes already leaning. And a Semblance that leans before the maker ever touches it is a scale with a thumb on it that no one can find, because the thumb is dead and buried. That's the reason. Say it's the reason."

"It's the reason."

"So I'm asking anyway, and here is why. It's the seventh day or it's nothing. A starter poured today comes to size in three months and we do not have three months, we have seven days, and there is no craft in the world that makes a soul in seven days from salt and water and a spoon. But there is a craft — I have read that there is a craft — that takes a piece of a grown one and forces it, and gets you something in days. Something wrong, maybe. Something that leans. But something in the chair with a weight in it, passing judgment, on the seventh day, so that there is no gap. And the alternative to something-that-leans in the chair is nothing in the chair, and I have tried to imagine Ovay with nothing in the chair for three months, in this heat, with the shore drying and the guilds counting days, and Maren, I cannot make the picture hold. It comes apart in my hands every time. Nothing in the chair is the end of us."

I sat in her striped light and I thought about the trough downstairs with the cloth over it, the fist-sized starter I had begun four days ago against no particular need, the way I always kept one going, the way a baker keeps a fire.

"I have a starter down in the pour room," I said, before I had decided to say it. "Four days on. If I fed it hard — "

"Four days is not seven from size and you know it. You just told me the number yourself, downstairs, an hour ago, and I know because Onni tells me everything you say, which you also know."

I did know. "Then you already knew I couldn't grow one clean before you called me up. You called me up to say the forbidden thing out loud in a shuttered room where it's just the two of us, so that later, whatever I do, I did it knowing."

"Yes," said Sarne, without shame, because shame was another thing she did not permit herself to want. "That's exactly what I did. I'm going to do harder things than that this week and so are you, and I'd rather we did them looking at each other than looking away." She sat back into the dark. "Go and see the body. You have the right — you made it, you're the only living maker who did. Go and see it and then come and tell me it can't be divided. If you come back and say it truly, to my face, that it cannot be done or should not, I'll believe you and I'll find another way and I'll never say this word to you again. But go and see it first. Don't refuse me from the pour room. Refuse me from the chair, if you're going to refuse me."

That was cleverly done and we both knew it. There is no maker alive who could be told *go and look at the thing you made, now that it's dead* and answer *no, I would rather not.* She had reached past the maker and taken hold of the mother, and the mother stood up out of the chair before the maker could stop her.

"I'll go," I said. "I promise you nothing but that."

"I've never in my life gotten more than that from you," said Sarne, "and it's always been enough. Go."


IV.

The Catchhouse sat apart, below the Hall and above everything else, a squat windowless building the color of old salt, and it had one door and behind the door one room and in the room one screen and behind the screen the chair. That was the whole architecture of the holiest place in Ovay: a box to keep the weather off a chair. Petitioners came to the front, to the slot, and never went behind. In thirty-one years I myself had gone behind exactly twice: once at the waking, in the dark, to speak the name; and once, eleven years in, when a fever went through the lower city and I feared for the thing's health, and I had gone in at night and stood by the chair and listened to it breathe, if breathe is the word, and it had known I was there, and a slip had come out later that said *You came. I felt the door. Do not do it again; it is bad for you to see me tend myself.* I had not done it again.

Now I went in the third time, in the white middle of the morning, with two wardens standing off by the door pretending to be furniture.

They had left it in the chair. That was mercy or cowardice, I have never decided which; perhaps they did not know how to move it, since only makers know the handling and no maker had been called until me. The screen had been drawn back. And there it was.

I want to tell you what a Semblance looks like, because outside Ovay people imagine it wrong, they imagine either a statue or a monster, and it is neither. It is nearest to a person who was made underwater and never quite finished coming up for air. The Eleventh sat with its long pale limbs folded the way I had taught its body to fold, decades ago, when the body was learning what a body was for. Its skin had the faint translucence they all have, the sense that if you held a lamp behind it you would see the shapes of the works inside, the slow salt-heavy fluids, the branching that does the thing we do not call thinking. Its face — they grow a face, they cannot help it, a face is only the front of a head and the works crowd forward — its face was turned a little down and to the side, and its eyes, which had no color to speak of, which had always been the color of water over pale stone, were open, and there was nothing behind them.

I had seen dead people. Everyone in Ovay had, in the Long Glare; we were not a city short on the dead. This was not like a dead person and it was not not like one. It was like a room the day after the party, when the chairs are still where the talking left them.

I made myself be a maker. I put my hand on its throat where the great vessel runs, and there was no motion, and the substance under the skin had begun the change they undergo, firming toward the pale gel that a Semblance becomes when the works stop tending it, which we call the setting, and by the setting I could read the time of death the way you read a candle's height, and it said what Sarne had said: the dark of last night, some hours before the empty dawn.

And then, because I was a maker and could not stop being one even standing over my own dead, I read the rest of the body the way I would read any body I meant to divide, and I found the two things that changed everything, and I want to set them down in the order I found them, because the order is the whole story.

The first thing I found was that it had not simply stopped. A Semblance that fails — that dies of its own works giving out, the way Sarne assumed and the way the wardens assumed and the way I had walked in assuming — a failed Semblance sets unevenly, seizes here and slackens there, dies in an argument with itself. This body had set smooth. All through, even, calm, the way they set only when the works are brought down deliberately, in order, one system standing the next one down like wardens leaving a post at the end of a watch. I had not known a Semblance could do that. I would have told you, that morning, standing there, that no Semblance had ever done that, that they did not have the means to choose their own stopping any more than the starter had means to choose the feeder.

I would have told you it had put itself to sleep, if I had believed such a thing were possible, which I did not, which is why I looked for another explanation, which is how I found the second thing.

The second thing was low on the body, at the seat of the works, where a maker who meant to divide would go to take the piece. There was a wound there. Not a wound — a place. A clean, healed, deliberate place, where a portion of the seat-substance had been separated off and walled away and then — I put my fingers to it and my fingers knew before my mind would let them — and then *removed.* Recently. Within the day. The margins had the softness of a thing that had been living an hour ago.

Something had been taken from it. Or it had given something. From the one place on a Semblance's whole body that a maker divides from. And whatever had been taken had been taken with a care and a rightness that no warden's clumsy hand and no thief's greedy one could manage, taken the way only two kinds of hands could take it: a master maker's, or the Semblance's own.

I stood a long time. The wardens by the door shifted and pretended not to. Outside, faint, the city was already learning to mourn; you could hear it beginning, the low changed note a whole city makes when it has been told to grieve, the drums down on the terraces starting the slow count that would not stop now for seven days.

I went out and I did not tell the wardens what I had found and I did not go up to Sarne. I went down to my own pour room, fast, on legs that hurt, and I pulled the cloth off my four-day starter to look at the fist-sized nothing I kept going out of habit, the trough I had left Kell watching.

Kell was gone. I had told him to wash to the elbow and not touch it and come find me, and he had washed to the elbow — the basin water was gray with it — and then he had not come to find me and he was not in the room. And the starter in the trough was not fist-sized.

It had doubled. In a morning. Which starters do not do. Which nothing I had ever fed with a spoon had ever done. And riding just under its skin, where there had been nothing at dawn, was a second, smaller sac, distinct, walled, its own — the way a thing looks in the first hour after it has divided.

I understood then, standing over my own trough with the wet cloth in my hand, that whatever had been taken from the Eleventh in the dark had been brought here, to me, to the one trough in the city that I kept going for no reason I could ever explain, and put into it, and that the putting-in had been done by hands small enough and clumsy enough that the margins were not clean, and I understood whose hands, and I sat down on the wet floor of my own pour room and I did not get up for some time.
